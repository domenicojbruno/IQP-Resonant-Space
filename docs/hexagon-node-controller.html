SHEET 1

This is now a power board

Rev A of this document assumed IBT-2 modules wired off-board, which kept motor current entirely off the PCB. The V2 schematic places ten bare BTS7960B chips on the board itself. That is a legitimate choice — it is more compact, cheaper per node, and removes twenty flying leads — but it inverts the central design constraint, so most of what follows changed with it.

Motor current now crosses your PCB. Three consequences you are committing to:

  • Copper is your heatsink. The BTS7960B in TO263-7 dissipates through its tab. Each conducting switch drops roughly I²·RDS(on), and at ~16 mΩ that's about 0.4 W at 5 A and 1.6 W at a 10 A stall — per switch, with two in the path at any moment. You need a real pour under every tab, not a trace.
  • Two-ounce copper, not one. At 1 oz the pours and the motor runs both have to be roughly twice as wide for the same temperature rise. The cost difference per board is small; the layout difference is not.
  • Ground becomes a layout problem, not a wiring problem. With modules, keeping motor return out of the logic reference was a matter of how you ran wires. On-board, it is a matter of how you draw pours — and a mistake there is a respin rather than a re-crimp.

What the board owns now: the ESP32, ten half-bridges and their gate-side passives, all connectors, the enable/disarm circuit, and the sensor conditioning. Sheet 9 covers the layout rules that follow from this.

Out of scope

ESP32 supply is handled separately and is not addressed here. The rails below cover the motor bus, the sensor rail, and the lighting rail only.

Protection you don't have yet

  • A fuse on J1. Ten half-bridges rated 43 A apiece will happily deliver whatever your supply can source into a fault. Nothing in firmware can prevent that.
  • Reverse-polarity protection. J1 is a bare two-pin terminal. Backwards once, on a board with ten unprotected power ICs, and the node is gone. A P-channel MOSFET in the high side costs almost nothing and drops far less than a Schottky.
  • 10 kΩ pull-down on DRV_EN. Covered on Sheet 4, but it belongs on this list too — it is a power-domain safety device, not a logic nicety.

Supply sizing

Five simultaneous stalls is the honest worst case and it is a large number. Rather than buy a supply for a state that should never occur, make it unreachable in firmware — slew-limit every duty change, cap concurrent motion, and stagger starts. A ripple is a staggered start by definition, so this costs nothing in the effects you actually want.

Then add 220–470 µF plus 100 nF at each chip's VS, physically adjacent. The datasheet requires local bulk capacitance; without it the switching edge is pulled through the whole supply path and the chips do not survive long.

GPIO12 is a trap, not a spare

GPIO12 is MTDI: held high at boot, the ESP32 sets its internal flash regulator to 1.8 V and fails to start. Both candidate signals fail here — an IR module idles HIGH whenever nothing is in front of it, and a limit switch is most likely to be closed at power-on, because that is the home position. Route nothing to this pin.

Prerequisite: rewire the limit switches

J7–J11 currently bridge 3.3 V → L_DATA. Change them to switch → GND and enable the internal pull-ups. That deletes five external resistors, satisfies GPIO15's boot requirement, and makes a cable chafed against the frame read as "at limit" rather than as a false idle.

The four input-only pins (36, 39, 34, 35) have no internal pull-ups of any kind, so each of those IR lines needs a 10 kΩ to 3.3 V on the board. The HW-201 drives its output actively; the resistor is there so an unplugged connector doesn't leave a floating input.

Zero duty is a brake, not a coast

Writing 0 to both PWM channels ties both actuator terminals to ground, which brakes. The only way to truly coast is to drop DRV_EN. This is fine — and during a direction reversal it is preferable, since braking decays winding current faster than coasting — but it is worth knowing that "stopped" and "released" are different commands on this topology.

Note there is no VCC pin on the bare chip. Wiring guides that show a logic supply are describing the IBT-2 module's header, not the IC. IN and INH are referenced to ground and take 3.3 V logic as-is, so no level shifting is needed anywhere on the motor side.

DRV_EN

One net to all ten INH pins gives a hardware kill that does not depend on the PWM peripheral or on firmware being sane: pull it low and every bridge goes high-impedance regardless of what the LEDC channels are doing. It covers a hung sketch, a misconfigured timer, and the boot window before setup() runs. Three things make it real:

  • A 10 kΩ pull-down on the PCB. Never rely on a GPIO's reset state. This resistor is what guarantees disarmed at power-on and disarmed when the ESP32 is unplugged.
  • An e-stop in series — a normally-closed mushroom button breaking the net, so opening it lets the pull-down disarm everything. Two-pin header on the board, jumpered for bench work.
  • A firmware watchdog that drops the line if the control loop misses its deadline.
Why the current-sense pins stay unpopulated

The BTS7960B reports output current at IS as a scaled current source, nominally 1:8500. The specified spread on that ratio is 3000 to 14000. Through a 1 kΩ resistor, a 3 A load therefore reads anywhere from 0.21 V to 1.00 V depending on which chip you happened to solder — a 4.7× uncertainty that no software calibration constant can fix across twenty chips.

Worse, the failure direction is dangerous. A stalled actuator at 10 A on a low-ratio chip produces 3.3 V, already at the ESP32's absolute maximum; at 20 A the pin is destroyed. Reading these safely would need a smaller sense resistor plus a 3.3 V zener clamp and series resistor.

Leave the pads unpopulated and detect stalls another way. Sheet 6 covers what is available without position feedback.

SHEET 5

Sensor interfaces

Proximity — HW-201 ×5

One module per hexagon on a 3-pin connector carrying 3.3 V, ground, and the comparator output. Power them from 3.3 V, not 5 V. The LM393 output swings to its supply rail and ESP32 pins are not 5 V tolerant, so a 5 V-fed module puts 5 V straight into the GPIO. At 3.3 V the comparator works fine and detection range shortens slightly, which you recover with the module's trimpot.

Treat each channel as active-low with time hysteresis: require the line to hold a new state for ~30 ms before accepting it. The comparator has no hysteresis of its own, so a target hovering at the threshold will chatter, and a hexagon that flutters looks worse than one that responds slowly.

Known limitation

The HW-201 is reflectivity-dependent — dark clothing absorbs IR and may not trigger it at all. On a campus, that is a large fraction of your audience. Budget for the alternative now: two spare pins broken out as an I²C header plus a TCA9548A footprint makes VL53L1X time-of-flight sensors a populate option rather than a respin.

Homing switches ×5

Wired switch-to-ground with internal pull-ups, as Sheet 3 requires. Add 1 kΩ series and 100 nF to ground at each input for debounce — mechanical switches bounce for several milliseconds, and an RC there is cheaper than the firmware to handle it. Debounce in software as well; both together is not redundant when the cable run is metres long inside a metal frame.

Lighting

J12 pin 1 is currently on VDD, the motor rail. WS2812s expect 5 V and 12 V destroys them immediately — this needs the separate 5 V converter from Sheet 2. Then add a 330 Ω series resistor on LED_DATA and 1000 µF across the strip's supply at the strip end.

One more mismatch: a 3.3 V data line into a 5 V WS2812 is marginal by spec and tends to work on the bench and fail in the installation. Either level-shift with a 74AHCT125, or use SK6812 strip, which tolerates 3.3 V logic reliably. If the strip exceeds roughly thirty LEDs, give it its own supply rather than the node converter — at full white these draw about 60 mA each.

SHEET 6

Position without position feedback

Rev A specified a potentiometer per scissor. V2 replaces them with one limit switch per actuator. These are not equivalent instruments, and the difference decides which effects the wall can perform — so it is worth stating plainly rather than discovering during integration.

A limit switch reports arrival at one end. A potentiometer reports where you are, continuously. With switches alone, the only position the node knows for certain is fully-retracted; everything else is inferred from how long a motor has been commanded to run.

The homing routine

At boot, and periodically during idle, drive each actuator toward its switch at low duty until the switch closes. That point is position zero. From there, track position by integrating commanded run time against a calibrated travel rate.

Four things make that estimate drift: supply sag under load, stiction varying with temperature and wear, duty-to-speed nonlinearity near the floor, and the ramp itself (the first and last 150 ms of every move cover less distance than the middle). None are correctable without feedback, all are bounded by re-homing.

  • Re-home on a schedule — every few minutes of idle, or between visitor interactions. Nobody notices a wall settling to rest; everybody notices one whose ripple has gone lopsided.
  • Re-home on surprise. If a switch closes when the model says the scissor is mid-stroke, the model is wrong. Trust the switch, reset the estimate, log it.
  • Bound the far end in time, not position. With one switch you have one reference; full extension has to be a time limit with margin, or you drive into the mechanical stop.
If you want it back later

An MCP23017 moves all ten digital inputs onto two I²C pins and frees enough GPIO to put five potentiometers directly on ADC1 — no multiplexer needed, because GPIO32/33 could then be released from motor duty. That is a board revision, not a rewrite, and the firmware change is confined to one sensor module. Leaving the I²C header from Sheet 5 on this board is what keeps that door open.

SHEET 7

End-stops that outlive the firmware

The GPIO switches on Sheet 5 tell the controller where home is. They do not protect anything — if firmware hangs mid-move, nothing reads them. For that, put a normally-closed switch in series with the actuator lead and bridge it with a diode oriented to pass current the other way.

This lives in the actuator harness, not on the PCB. The diodes carry full motor current, so use a Schottky rated well above stall — an SB540 for a typical 12 V unit, larger if your actuators draw more.

Because the same switch cannot be both in the motor's power path and readable by a 3.3 V GPIO, doing both at one end needs either a DPDT microswitch — one pole inline, one to the ESP32 — or two switches. The DPDT is the tidier answer and costs about a dollar more.

Check the actuator first

Most commercial 12 V linear actuators ship with exactly this circuit inside the housing. If yours do, skip it: the actuator cannot destroy itself, and your remaining risk is the scissor linkage binding before the actuator reaches its stroke end. Confirm the model before buying switches and diodes.

SHEET 9

Layout guidance

This is where the bare-chip decision is paid for. Rev A could be laid out carelessly and still work; this version cannot.

Power

  • 2 oz copper. The upcharge is small and it roughly halves the width needed for a given temperature rise.
  • Pour under every tab. The TO263-7 dissipates through its tab into the board. Give each chip a continuous pour of at least a square inch, and stitch it with thermal vias if you go four-layer. Ten chips in a row can share a pour per net, which is both easier and better.
  • Pours, not traces, for V_MOT and motor ground. Route the actuator outputs as pours or wide polygons to the terminals; do not neck them down at a connector pad.
  • Bulk caps within ~10 mm of their VS pin, with a short, wide return. A correctly chosen capacitor 40 mm away is doing much less than you think.
  • Space the chips for heat. Ten packages shoulder to shoulder share one thermal mass and reach a much higher steady state than any one of them alone.

Ground

  • Two pours, joined once, at the input terminal. Motor return on one, logic and sensor return on the other. Do not let the join happen incidentally under the ESP32.
  • Keep PWM routing off the motor pours. Ten switching gate signals crossing a pour that is carrying tens of amps of switched current will couple. Run them on the logic side.
  • Sensor connectors together, away from the power section, with their conditioning passives local to them.

Build and service

  • Socket the ESP32. Female headers. These die and get swapped, and a student team will thank you.
  • Silkscreen for someone on a ladder. Every connector labelled with its net and motor number — M3 IR, M3 LIMIT, M3 OUT — legible at arm's length. Twenty near-identical connectors per wall is how a miswire happens.
  • Three rail LEDs. V_MOT, 5 V, 3.3 V. Turns most bring-up questions into a glance.
  • Test points on DRV_EN, each rail, and at least one OUT.
  • Mounting holes on your extrusion pitch, M4 clearance, landing on the 80/20 rails already in the build.
SHEET 10

Open items before fabrication

The pin map and topology are settled. These affect component ratings, copper width, and firmware constants — not the logic design.

  • Actuator make and model. Still the top of the list. Voltage, stall current, stroke, full-travel time — and whether it has internal end-stops, which decides whether Sheet 7 gets built. Stall current also sets the fuse, the pour widths, and the supply.
  • Confirm the ESP32 module variant. GPIO16/17 carry two limit switches in this map and are consumed internally by PSRAM on a WROVER. On a WROVER, move them to GPIO12… which is unusable, so a WROVER would force the MCP23017 from Sheet 6.
  • Travel time per scissor, retracted to extended, at a known duty. This is the calibration constant the entire dead-reckoning model rests on.
  • LED strip length and type. Decides whether the node converter can feed it and whether you need the level shifter or SK6812s.
  • Scissors per wall. The BOM says 13 per wall; five per node implies 20 across four nodes. This changes the node count and the Phase 4 topology.
Firmware status

Phase 1 motor bring-up is written and matches this pin map exactly — the motor pairs are unchanged from Rev A, so no code edit is needed. Phases 2 and 3 will need the homing routine and drift model from Sheet 6, which did not exist in the Rev A plan.
